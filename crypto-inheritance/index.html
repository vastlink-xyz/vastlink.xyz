What is crypto inheritance planning?

Crypto inheritance planning makes sure the right people can access your bitcoin and other digital assets after you die, and that nobody can access them while you're alive. A complete plan covers three things: keeping control of your keys during your lifetime, making sure your heirs can find what you left, and giving them a way to actually move the funds. Without a plan, crypto held in self-custody is usually lost for good when its owner dies.

A complete crypto inheritance plan has to solve three things

1

Nobody can take it while you're alive

Your keys stay with you. Pieces are placed so that nobody (your heirs included) can reach the threshold without you, and your own backup is released only under your will. No company holds a spare key, and there's no “emergency co-sign” backdoor.

2

Your family can find it

Your family knows where things are, who to contact and what to do first. “The bitcoin is on my Trezor” doesn't help someone who isn't technical.

3

They can actually get it out

Your heirs shouldn't need to understand private keys or use a command line. They need steps written in order, a person they can call, and a setup where one mistake can't lock the funds away forever.

Most inheritance plans solve only the first problem, or only the second. Securing your keys without a way for heirs to use them is like locking your money in a safe that only you can open.

How it works

  1. 01

    Set up (wallet in minutes, full plan in one sitting)

    Creating the wallet takes a few minutes in a free mobile app. Control is split into pieces, for example 3-of-4: you hold two (one on your phone, one backup in your own safe), and one heir and your lawyer hold one each. No single piece can move funds, and your heirs can't reach the threshold without you. The rest of the sitting (about two hours) goes on the key-split and beneficiary plan, storing the backup pieces in separate places, and the family rehearsal.

  2. 02

    Maintain (a check-in every 6 months)

    Every 6 months we remind you to confirm three things: the pieces are still where they should be, the beneficiary list is up to date, and your family can still find the handbook.

  3. 03

    Execute (no technical skills needed)

    Your family uses the same free mobile app: open it on two phones and approve with a QR code. A short handbook covers what the app can't: who to contact, where the pieces are, and what proof releases your backup. They don't have to read it for the first time on the day it matters, because during setup we walk your heirs through the whole process once, with you there.

FAQ

Can't I just set up multisig myself with Sparrow?

Yes, as long as you'll always be around, always clear-headed, and willing to write a handbook for your family. We don't sell multisig. Multisig is free and open source, and you can set it up yourself whenever you like. What we sell is this: on the day something happens to you, your family has a document they can follow and a person they can ask. The technology isn't the hard part. Carrying out the plan is.

If the software is open source, what am I paying for?

Not the software. Anyone can use it for free, and so can you. You pay for the parts that aren't code: choosing who holds which piece so your heirs can't act without you, writing down where the backups are and what proof releases them, running a rehearsal with your family, and keeping all of it current when a device is lost, a piece is reissued, or someone joins or leaves the family. If you'd rather do that yourself, Self-Guided gives you the templates.

Will you hold any of my keys?

No, not a single one. This is where we differ from Casa and Unchained, who each hold a backup key for recovery or emergency co-signing. Our setup has no Vastlink key at all. The trade-off is that we can't recover your funds for you, which is why every setup includes a real rehearsal.

When I die, how will my family know who to contact?

That's why the first step of setup is a list of people, not technology: who is the executor, who helps with the technical side, and who is the backup contact. The list goes in the handbook. Your will points to the handbook, never to a seed phrase.

Frequently asked questions

Can my family inherit my crypto if they can't log in to my wallet?

Yes. There's nothing to log in to and no seed phrase to find. Control is split into pieces kept on different devices, and no complete key ever exists. That's why a plan has to state which pieces are held by whom and in what order they are combined. The full procedure goes in a handbook written for your heirs.

Should I put my seed phrase in my will?

It's the most common approach and the most dangerous one. After death, a will usually goes through probate, and many people may see it. Anyone who sees a seed phrase can take everything. Your will should say who inherits and where to find the pieces, not the seed phrase itself.

How is this different from a crypto trust?

A trust deals with legal title: who is legally entitled to inherit. A key-split plan deals with technical execution: how your family actually gets the coins. They don't conflict, and you need both. If you already have a trust or a will, this plan is the execution layer underneath it.

I hold ETH and stablecoins, not bitcoin. Does this work for me?

Yes. The same inheritance logic applies to Bitcoin, Ethereum and the major stablecoins (USDT, USDC). Tell us what you hold during the gap review and we'll confirm the setup for each asset.

Do I need to be technical?

No. Creating the wallet takes a few minutes in a mobile app, and approving a transfer means scanning a QR code with a second phone. The app handles the cryptography. What it can't decide for you is who holds which piece, where the backups are kept, and what your family does first. That's what the plan and the short handbook cover, tested in a real rehearsal.

Could someone claim my crypto while I'm still alive?

No. No single piece can move funds, and there is no company backup key. Pieces are placed so your heirs can't reach the threshold without you. For example, in a 3-of-4 setup you hold two pieces (your phone and a backup in your own safe), so an heir and your lawyer together hold only two of the three needed. Your backup is released to your executor under your will.

What happens if Vastlink shuts down?

Nothing changes for your coins. We never hold a key, the tools are open source, and the handbook only uses software your family can get for free. Signing happens between your own devices: a relay can pass encrypted messages between phones that are far apart, but it can't move funds, it can be self-hosted, and phones on the same WiFi can sign without it. Your plan keeps working without us.

Isn't a social recovery wallet enough?

Social recovery helps you get back into your own wallet if you lose a device. Inheritance is a different problem: someone else needs access after you're gone, with legal authority and instructions they can follow. A recovery setup can be part of an inheritance plan, but on its own it isn't one.
